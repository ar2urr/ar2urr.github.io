Problem

Malware authors make small structural edits so variants slip past signature matching. Graph edit distance can see through that, but results depend heavily on how each edit is priced.

Approach

A from-scratch Python pipeline that builds block-level CFGs from bytecode, normalises them, and computes exact GED under a uniform and a role-weighted cost model.

Result

9 of 11 comparisons classified correctly. Every topology-preserving obfuscation scored exactly 0. Role-weighted costs raised distances by 54% on average without changing a single verdict.

Normalisation makes graphs from different compilations comparable before any distance is computed. Each block gets a structural role (entry, branch, merge, exit or linear), linear chains are collapsed, and nodes are relabelled in canonical breadth-first order. Chain collapsing is what takes no-op insertion and expression splitting down to a distance of exactly 0.

Exact GED is NP-hard, so the search runs A* with a Hungarian-assignment lower bound, tightened by out-degree imbalance, against Dijkstra as the uninformed baseline. Both run in parallel threads and their results are asserted equal. A* pruned up to 86.6% of states but lost 83% of the races, because the per-state Hungarian cost dominates on graphs this small.

The role-weighted model prices branch nodes at 2.5, entry 2.0, merge 1.5, exit 1.2 and linear 1.0, jump edges at 2.0 and fallthroughs at 1.0, and halves every insertion, since obfuscation mostly adds structure rather than removing it. Distances are size-normalised before a 0.75 variant threshold is applied.

The main limitation is scale: twelve hand-built samples from one baseline function, with the threshold calibrated on the same data. The natural next step is a real corpus such as MalNet-Graph.
